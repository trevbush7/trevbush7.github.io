Research Interests

My work is grounded in network pharmacology: defining how lipid and protein interaction networks are disrupted in aging and chronic inflammatory disease, and designing polypharmacological interventions that restore those networks toward healthier system-level states.

1. Multi-Omics Systems Biology

Quantitative Mapping of Lipid–Protein Networks in Aging and Inflammation

I use a multi-omics systems biology framework to characterize how lipid metabolism and signaling networks become disorganized across tissues, cell types, and anatomical niches during aging and chronic inflammatory disease. This framework is explicitly anchored in mass spectrometry–based lipidomics and proteomics, with RNA sequencing and spatial omics providing the regulatory and anatomical context needed to interpret these changes at the network level.

  • Lipidomics (LC–MS/MS and imaging): High-resolution LC–MS/MS lipidomics to quantify sphingolipids, glycerophospholipids, neutral lipids, and oxidized species, coupled with lipid class– and species–level ratio analysis to infer in vivo changes in enzymatic activity and pathway flux (e.g., ceramide/S1P balance, remodeling of membrane lipids, and storage lipid turnover) across aging and inflammatory states.
  • Proteomics (DIA/DDA, PTM and turnover profiling): Global and targeted MS-based proteomics (including DIA approaches and PTM-focused workflows) to quantify metabolic enzymes, lipid-handling proteins, receptors, and signaling intermediates, as well as their post-translational modifications and turnover. This layer defines the protein interaction and reaction nodes that implement lipid network behavior.
  • RNA sequencing: Bulk and sorted-population RNA-seq to capture transcriptional programs controlling lipid metabolism, inflammatory tone, and stress responses. RNA-seq provides the regulatory scaffold that explains why specific enzymes and pathways are up- or down-regulated, but is interpreted in the context of the lipid and protein readouts that directly encode cell state.
  • Spatial omics (MALDI / DESI and related imaging): Mass spectrometry imaging and related spatial omics platforms to map the tissue- and niche-specific localization of lipids and proteins within intact organs. These methods allow me to compare normal physiology, aging, and pathology, and then determine how candidate interventions drive spatially resolved remodeling back toward healthier phenotypes at key interfaces and compartments.

I prioritize these layers—lipidomics, proteomics, RNA-seq, and spatial omics— because they provide complementary views of the same underlying lipid–protein network architecture: lipids and proteins as the direct effectors, RNA as the upstream regulatory code, and spatial omics as the anatomical map. Other omics such as epigenomics, metabolomics, or single-cell chromatin profiling are incorporated selectively when specific mechanistic questions demand them, but the core of my work focuses on the effector-level and spatially grounded readouts that most directly capture network function.

By integrating these omics layers, I aim to build network models that explain how lipid remodeling, protein network rewiring, and niche-specific localization jointly determine vulnerability or resilience in aging and chronic inflammatory disease—and to identify actionable network nodes and motifs that can be targeted to restore homeostasis.

2. Herbal Systems Pharmacology

Network Pharmacology, Polypharmacology, and Synergy in Herbal Medicine

The second arm of my research applies herbal systems pharmacology to these network maps. Rather than focusing on single-target inhibition, I study how multi-target natural products and rational herbal combinations reshape lipid–protein interaction networks. This explicitly adopts a network pharmacology perspective—modeling each compound or formula as a distributed input into a complex biological graph.

  • Polypharmacology mapping with mass spectrometry and CETSA-MS: Using quantitative lipidomics, proteomics, and CETSA-MS and related developments to define direct and indirect target engagement of herbal constituents across the proteome and lipidome. This enables construction of compound–target–pathway networks that reveal how individual molecules influence multiple enzymes, receptors, transporters, and signaling hubs within lipid and inflammatory circuits.
  • Synergy and network-level response profiling: Systematic testing of herbal combinations, fractions, and dose matrices, analyzed with multi-omic readouts, to detect non-additive, network-level responses. Rather than defining synergy solely by a viability or signaling readout, I quantify how combinations cooperatively normalize multi-node lipid and protein signatures associated with aging and chronic inflammation.
  • Spatially resolved intervention mapping: Leveraging spatial omics (MALDI, DESI, and related imaging methods) to determine where, within intact tissues and interfaces, herbal interventions restore lipid and protein localization patterns toward a younger or healthier spatial phenotype. This connects molecular mechanisms to specific anatomical niches (e.g., barrier regions, vascular interfaces, stem/progenitor niches) that are critical for long-term tissue function.
  • Network-guided lead selection and mechanism-of-action: Integrating compound–target data, multi-omic signatures, and spatial changes into network pharmacology models that rank natural products and combinations by their ability to rewire disease networks back toward homeostatic attractor states. This provides a mechanistically grounded path from complex herbal mixtures to prioritized leads and testable mechanisms.

Together, the multi-omics systems biology and herbal systems pharmacology programs form a closed loop: I first use lipidomics, proteomics, RNA-seq, and spatial omics to define how networks fail in aging and chronic inflammatory disease, and then apply polypharmacological and synergistic herbal interventions—quantified by MS-based and CETSA-MS approaches—to identify strategies that robustly restore those networks and their spatial organization toward healthier states.

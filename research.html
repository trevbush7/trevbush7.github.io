Research Interests

My work is grounded in network pharmacology: defining how lipid composition, protein signaling networks, and transcriptional programs become dysregulated in aging and chronic inflammatory disease, and designing multi-target interventions that restore these networks—and their spatial organization—toward healthier system-level states.

I use a network-guided phenotypic strategy. First, I map lipid–protein network dysfunction using multi-omics profiling. Natural products and herbal combinations are then screened for their ability to restore these system-level phenotypes. Proteome-wide structural and stability profiling platforms (CETSA-MS, LiP-MS, PELSA, and related approaches) are applied under mixture conditions to define how multi-component formulas perturb protein networks. These measurements are integrated with lipidomics, proteomics, RNA sequencing, and spatial omics to resolve distributed mechanisms of polypharmacology and emergent synergy.

1. Multi-Omics Systems Biology

Effector-Layer and Spatial Mapping of Lipid–Protein Networks

I use a multi-omics systems biology framework to map how lipid metabolism and signaling networks reorganize across tissues, cell types, and anatomical niches during normal physiology, aging, and chronic inflammatory disease. This framework integrates mass spectrometry–based lipidomics and proteomics with RNA sequencing, cell-type–resolved profiling, and spatial omics, allowing regulatory, cellular, and anatomical context to be interpreted together at the network level.

  • Lipidomics (LC–MS/MS and imaging): High-resolution LC–MS/MS lipidomics is used to quantify complex lipid species, with class- and species-level ratio analysis to infer in vivo changes in enzymatic activity and pathway flux. These measurements define how lipid composition and bioactive lipid mediators shift across aging and inflammatory states.
  • Proteomics (DIA-MS, protein abundance, PTM, and phosphoproteomics): Quantitative data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry is used to measure proteome-wide protein abundance with high reproducibility across tissues, cell types, and conditions. This is coupled with targeted and global post-translational modification profiling—including phosphoproteomics—to define kinase activity, inflammatory pathway engagement, protein turnover, and post-translational regulation of lipid metabolic enzymes. Together, these measurements resolve both the quantitative architecture and signaling state of protein networks governing lipid remodeling.
  • Cell-type–resolved profiling (flow cytometry and FACS): Flow cytometric phenotyping and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) are used to isolate defined cell populations prior to lipidomic, proteomic, phosphoproteomic, and transcriptomic analysis. This makes it possible to distinguish changes in tissue composition from intrinsic lipid–protein network rewiring within specific immune, stromal, epithelial, or progenitor populations.
  • RNA sequencing: Bulk and sorted-population RNA-seq captures transcriptional programs that regulate lipid metabolism, stress responses, and inflammatory signaling. These data provide the regulatory context for interpreting lipidomic and proteomic changes, linking network remodeling to upstream gene-expression control.
  • Spatial omics (MALDI / DESI and related imaging): Mass spectrometry imaging and related spatial omics platforms are used to map the tissue localization of lipids and proteins within intact organs. These approaches allow direct comparison of molecular organization across physiology, aging, and pathology, and then assessment of how candidate interventions shift these patterns toward a younger or healthier phenotype.

These layers—lipidomics, proteomics (including abundance, PTM, and phosphoproteomics), RNA-seq, cell-type–resolved profiling, and spatial omics—capture the effector layer of biological systems (lipids and proteins), its regulatory control (transcription), and its spatial organization. Other omics, such as epigenomics or metabolomics, are incorporated when specific mechanistic questions require them, but the core framework focuses on functionally interpretable readouts that directly encode network behavior.

Bulk tissue profiling defines organ-level network remodeling; FACS-resolved multi-omics quantifies cell-intrinsic rewiring; and spatial omics preserves tissue architecture, revealing interface- and niche-specific changes that are obscured in homogenates. Integrating these data streams enables network models that explain how lipid remodeling, protein signaling state, and spatial organization jointly determine vulnerability or resilience in aging and chronic inflammatory disease, and highlights actionable network nodes and motifs for therapeutic intervention.

2. Herbal Systems Pharmacology

Network Pharmacology, Polypharmacology, and Synergy in Herbal Medicine

The second arm of my research applies herbal systems pharmacology to these network maps. Rather than optimizing single-target inhibition, I study how multi-target natural products and rational herbal combinations reshape lipid–protein interaction networks. Each compound or formula is modeled as a distributed perturbation to a biological graph, influencing multiple nodes simultaneously. To define these effects, I combine proteome-wide target-engagement platforms with lipidomics, proteomics, RNA sequencing, and spatial omics to determine how herbal perturbations propagate through regulatory, signaling, and metabolic layers of disease-relevant networks.

  • Proteome-wide stability and structural profiling (CETSA-MS, LiP-MS, PELSA): Proteome-wide stability and structural profiling platforms are applied under mixture conditions to define the distributed proteomic perturbation landscape induced by multi-component herbal formulas. Ligand-dependent shifts in thermal stability, conformational accessibility, and protein interaction states are quantified across the proteome, capturing both direct binding events and indirect network propagation. Rather than reducing formulas to isolated targets, these data are integrated to reconstruct polypharmacological network rewiring and identify cooperatively engaged protein modules that underlie emergent synergy and systems-level phenotype restoration.
  • Integration with lipidomics, proteomics, and RNA sequencing: Proteome-wide perturbation data are integrated with lipidomic, phosphoproteomic, and RNA-seq signatures to determine how protein modules propagate through lipid metabolic, signaling, and transcriptional circuits. This connects mixture-induced protein perturbations to downstream lipid remodeling, pathway activation, and gene-expression programs governing cellular state.
  • Synergy and network-level response profiling: Herbal combinations, fractions, and dose matrices are systematically tested with multi-omic readouts to identify non-additive network responses. Synergy is defined not only by single endpoints such as viability, but by cooperative normalization of lipid, protein, and transcriptional signatures associated with aging and chronic inflammation.
  • Spatially resolved intervention mapping: Spatial omics platforms (MALDI, DESI, and related imaging) are used to determine where, within intact tissues and interfaces, herbal interventions restore lipid and protein localization patterns toward a younger or healthier spatial phenotype. Where appropriate, these data are paired with spatially informed transcriptomic measurements to connect anatomical remodeling to local regulatory-state changes, linking mechanism to functionally important niches such as barrier regions, vascular interfaces, and stem/progenitor compartments.
  • Network-guided lead selection and mechanism-of-action: Mixture-induced proteomic perturbations, multi-omic signatures, and spatial remodeling are integrated into network pharmacology models that rank natural products and combinations by their ability to rewire disease networks toward homeostatic attractor states. This provides a mechanistically grounded path from complex herbal mixtures to prioritized leads and experimentally testable mechanisms of action.

Together, multi-omics systems biology and herbal systems pharmacology form a closed experimental loop. I first define how lipid–protein networks fail in aging and chronic disease using lipidomics, proteomics, RNA-seq, cell-type–resolved profiling, and spatial omics. Polypharmacological herbal interventions are then evaluated using CETSA-MS, LiP-MS, PELSA, multi-omic profiling, and spatial readouts to identify strategies that restore network structure and spatial organization toward healthier physiological states.

Research Interests

My work is grounded in network pharmacology: defining how lipid and protein interaction networks become miswired in aging and chronic inflammatory disease, and designing polypharmacological interventions that restore those networks—and their spatial organization—toward healthier system-level states.

1. Multi-Omics Systems Biology

Effector-Layer and Spatial Mapping of Lipid–Protein Networks

I use a multi-omics systems biology framework to characterize how lipid metabolism and signaling networks are reorganized across tissues, cell types, and anatomical niches during normal physiology, aging, and chronic inflammatory disease. This framework is anchored in mass spectrometry–based lipidomics and proteomics, with RNA sequencing, cell-type–resolved profiling, and spatial omics providing the regulatory, cellular, and anatomical context needed to interpret these changes at the network level.

  • Lipidomics (LC–MS/MS and imaging): High-resolution LC–MS/MS lipidomics to quantify complex lipid species (including sphingolipids, glycerophospholipids, neutral lipids, and oxidized lipids), combined with class- and species-level ratio analysis to infer in vivo changes in enzymatic activity and pathway flux. These measurements define how lipid effector networks—such as ceramide/S1P balance and membrane remodeling—shift across aging and inflammatory states.
  • Proteomics (DIA-MS, PTM, and phosphoproteomics): Quantitative data-independent acquisition (DIA) proteomics to achieve reproducible, proteome-wide coverage across tissues and conditions, coupled with targeted and global PTM profiling—including phosphoproteomics—to define kinase signaling activity, inflammatory pathway engagement, and post-translational regulation of lipid metabolic enzymes. This layer resolves how protein interaction and reaction nodes are dynamically rewired in aging and chronic inflammatory disease.
  • Cell-type–resolved profiling (flow cytometry and FACS): Flow cytometric phenotyping and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to isolate defined cell populations prior to lipidomic, proteomic, phosphoproteomic, and transcriptomic analysis. This makes it possible to distinguish compositional shifts in tissue architecture from intrinsic lipid–protein network rewiring within specific immune, stromal, epithelial, or progenitor populations.
  • RNA sequencing: Bulk and sorted-population RNA-seq to capture transcriptional programs that regulate lipid metabolism, stress responses, and inflammatory tone. RNA-seq provides the regulatory scaffold that explains why particular enzymes, transporters, and signaling modules are up- or down-regulated, but is always interpreted in the context of the lipid and protein effectors that directly implement cell state.
  • Spatial omics (MALDI / DESI and related imaging): Mass spectrometry imaging and related spatial omics platforms to map the tissue- and niche-specific localization of lipids and proteins within intact organs. These approaches allow direct comparison of molecular localization and remodeling under normal physiology, aging, and pathology, and then assessment of how candidate interventions shift these spatial patterns back toward a younger or healthier phenotype.

I prioritize these layers—lipidomics, proteomics (including PTM and phosphoproteomics), RNA-seq, cell-type–resolved profiling, and spatial omics— because together they capture the effector layer (lipids and proteins), its regulatory control (transcripts), and its anatomical deployment (spatial localization). Other omics, such as epigenomics or metabolomics, are incorporated as targeted follow-ups when specific mechanistic questions demand them, but the core of my work focuses on functionally and spatially grounded readouts that directly encode network behavior.

Bulk tissue profiling defines organ-level network remodeling; FACS-resolved multi-omics quantifies cell-intrinsic rewiring; and spatial omics preserves architecture, revealing interface- and niche-specific changes that cannot be seen in homogenates. Integrating these data streams enables network models that explain how lipid remodeling, protein signaling state, and spatial organization jointly determine vulnerability or resilience in aging and chronic inflammatory disease, and highlights actionable network nodes and motifs that can be targeted to restore homeostasis.

2. Herbal Systems Pharmacology

Network Pharmacology, Polypharmacology, and Synergy in Herbal Medicine

The second arm of my research applies herbal systems pharmacology to these network maps. Instead of optimizing single-target inhibition, I study how multi-target natural products and rational herbal combinations reshape lipid–protein interaction networks at scale. This adopts an explicit network pharmacology perspective: each compound or formula is modeled as a distributed input into a complex biological graph, influencing multiple nodes and edges simultaneously.

  • Proteome-wide target engagement (CETSA-MS, LiP-MS, PELSA): Complementary mass spectrometry–based structural and stability profiling approaches to define direct and indirect target engagement of herbal constituents. CETSA-MS quantifies ligand-induced thermal stabilization across the proteome; LiP-MS detects conformational and protease-accessibility changes associated with binding; and PELSA-type stability platforms resolve ligand-dependent shifts in protein folding and interaction states. Together, these methods generate high-confidence compound–target–pathway networks that map polypharmacology at proteome scale.
  • Integration with lipidomics and signaling networks: Target engagement data are integrated with lipidomic, proteomic, and phosphoproteomic signatures to determine how specific protein interactions propagate through lipid metabolic and inflammatory circuits. This connects individual binding events to emergent changes in lipid remodeling, signaling cascades, and effector programs at the network level.
  • Synergy and network-level response profiling: Systematic testing of herbal combinations, fractions, and dose matrices with multi-omic readouts to identify non-additive, network-level responses. Synergy is defined not only by single endpoints such as viability, but by cooperative normalization of multi-node lipid and protein signatures associated with aging and chronic inflammation.
  • Spatially resolved intervention mapping: Leveraging spatial omics (MALDI, DESI, and related imaging) to determine where, within intact tissues and interfaces, herbal interventions restore lipid and protein localization patterns toward a younger or healthier spatial phenotype. This links molecular mechanisms to specific anatomical niches—such as barrier regions, vascular interfaces, and stem/progenitor compartments—that are critical for long-term tissue function.
  • Network-guided lead selection and mechanism-of-action: Integrating compound–target engagement, multi-omic signatures, and spatial remodeling into network pharmacology models that rank natural products and combinations by their ability to rewire disease networks back toward homeostatic attractor states. This provides a mechanistically grounded path from complex herbal mixtures to prioritized leads and experimentally testable mechanisms of action.

Together, the multi-omics systems biology and herbal systems pharmacology programs form a closed loop: I first use lipidomics, proteomics (including PTM and phosphoproteomics), RNA-seq, cell-type–resolved profiling, and spatial omics to define how networks fail in aging and chronic inflammatory disease. I then apply polypharmacological and synergistic herbal interventions—quantified by MS-based lipid and protein profiling, CETSA-MS, LiP-MS, PELSA, and spatial readouts—to identify strategies that robustly restore network structure and spatial organization toward healthier phenotypes.

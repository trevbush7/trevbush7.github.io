Interests

Defining how lipid–protein networks become dysregulated in aging and chronic inflammatory disease — and designing multi-target interventions that restore them toward healthier states.

My work is grounded in network pharmacology: defining how lipid composition, protein signaling networks, and transcriptional programs become dysregulated in aging and chronic inflammatory disease, and designing multi-target interventions that restore these networks — and their spatial organization — toward healthier system-level states.

I use a network-guided phenotypic strategy. First, I map lipid–protein network dysfunction using multi-omics profiling. Natural products and herbal combinations are then screened for their ability to restore these system-level phenotypes. Proteome-wide structural and stability profiling platforms (CETSA-MS, LiP-MS, PELSA, and related approaches) are applied under mixture conditions to define how multi-component formulas perturb protein networks. These measurements are integrated with lipidomics, proteomics, RNA sequencing, and spatial omics to resolve distributed mechanisms of polypharmacology and emergent synergy.

The framework bridges reductionist target identification with emergent, whole-system therapeutic mechanisms — the conceptual backbone of my approach to polypharmacological herbal intervention.

1 — Multi-Omics Systems Biology ▾

Effector-Layer and Spatial Mapping of Lipid–Protein Networks

I use a multi-omics systems biology framework to map how lipid metabolism and signaling networks reorganize across tissues, cell types, and anatomical niches during normal physiology, aging, and chronic inflammatory disease. This framework integrates mass spectrometry–based lipidomics and proteomics with RNA sequencing, cell-type–resolved profiling, and spatial omics, allowing regulatory, cellular, and anatomical context to be interpreted together at the network level.

  • Lipidomics (LC–MS/MS and imaging) High-resolution LC–MS/MS lipidomics is used to quantify complex lipid species, with class- and species-level ratio analysis to infer in vivo changes in enzymatic activity and pathway flux. These measurements define how lipid composition and bioactive lipid mediators shift across aging and inflammatory states. Bulk tissue profiling is complemented by single-cell and cell-type–resolved lipidomics, enabling cell-intrinsic lipid network rewiring to be distinguished from changes driven by shifts in tissue composition.

    Cell-type–resolved lipidomics resolves the lipidome across individual, defined cell populations — making it possible to attribute lipid network rewiring to specific immune, stromal, or progenitor cell types rather than bulk tissue averages. This approach is central to distinguishing intrinsic lipidome changes from those driven by shifts in tissue composition during aging or disease.

    Lipidomics analysis pipeline — LipidSig 2.0

Its unified interface handles everything from data quality control and lipid ID conversion through differential expression, enrichment analysis, and machine learning — substantially lowering the barrier to systems-level lipid network interpretation.

  • Proteomics (DIA-MS, protein abundance, PTM, and phosphoproteomics) Quantitative data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry is used to measure proteome-wide protein abundance with high reproducibility across tissues, cell types, and conditions. This is coupled with targeted and global post-translational modification profiling — including phosphoproteomics — to define kinase activity, inflammatory pathway engagement, protein turnover, and post-translational regulation of lipid metabolic enzymes.
  • Cell-type–resolved profiling (flow cytometry and FACS) Flow cytometric phenotyping and FACS are used to isolate defined cell populations prior to lipidomic, proteomic, phosphoproteomic, and transcriptomic analysis. This makes it possible to distinguish changes in tissue composition from intrinsic lipid–protein network rewiring within specific immune, stromal, epithelial, or progenitor populations.
  • RNA sequencing Bulk and sorted-population RNA-seq captures transcriptional programs that regulate lipid metabolism, stress responses, and inflammatory signaling. These data provide the regulatory context for interpreting lipidomic and proteomic changes, linking network remodeling to upstream gene-expression control.
  • Spatial omics (MALDI / DESI and related imaging) Mass spectrometry imaging and related spatial omics platforms are used to map the tissue localization of lipids and proteins within intact organs. These approaches allow direct comparison of molecular organization across physiology, aging, and pathology, and assessment of how candidate interventions shift these patterns toward a younger or healthier phenotype.

    This workflow couples Raman spectroscopy with desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (DESI-MS) to simultaneously acquire chemical and lipid spatial maps from intact, cryo-sectioned tissue. Applied here to a mouse cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury model, it resolves the spatial redistribution of specific fatty acids, ceramides, phosphatidylserines, and sulfatides across brain regions — demonstrating that lipid dysregulation in disease is not uniform but is tightly organized within anatomical niches. Such spatial resolution is essential for interpreting how lipid network remodeling relates to regional vulnerability and pathology.

Bulk tissue profiling defines organ-level network remodeling; FACS-resolved multi-omics quantifies cell-intrinsic rewiring; and spatial omics preserves tissue architecture, revealing interface- and niche-specific changes that are obscured in homogenates. Integrating these data streams enables network models that explain how lipid remodeling, protein signaling state, and spatial organization jointly determine vulnerability or resilience in aging and chronic inflammatory disease.

2 — Herbal Systems Pharmacology ▾

Network Pharmacology, Polypharmacology, and Synergy in Herbal Medicine

The second arm of my research applies herbal systems pharmacology to these network maps. Rather than optimizing single-target inhibition, I study how multi-target natural products and rational herbal combinations reshape lipid–protein interaction networks. Each compound or formula is modeled as a distributed perturbation to a biological graph, influencing multiple nodes simultaneously.

  • Proteome-wide stability and structural profiling (CETSA-MS, LiP-MS, PELSA) These platforms are applied under mixture conditions to define the distributed proteomic perturbation landscape induced by multi-component herbal formulas. Ligand-dependent shifts in thermal stability, conformational accessibility, and protein interaction states are quantified across the proteome, capturing both direct binding events and indirect network propagation.

    CETSA exploits the principle that a ligand-bound protein is thermodynamically more stable than its unbound counterpart — and therefore resists heat-induced denaturation at higher temperatures. By measuring protein abundance remaining in the soluble fraction across a temperature gradient, CETSA reports on target engagement in intact cells or tissues. Scaled to the proteome via mass spectrometry (CETSA-MS), this approach maps the full complement of proteins whose thermal stability shifts in response to a compound or mixture, capturing both direct binding events and downstream conformational changes propagated through protein interaction networks.

    Figure from: Frida Niss, F., & Nordlund, P. (2025). From cells to organisms: CETSA applications in drug discovery and beyond. International Journal of Molecular Sciences, 26(9), Article 3940. https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26093940 ↗ Licensed under CC BY 4.0.

  • Integration with lipidomics, proteomics, and RNA sequencing Proteome-wide perturbation data are integrated with lipidomic, phosphoproteomic, and RNA-seq signatures to determine how protein modules propagate through lipid metabolic, signaling, and transcriptional circuits.
  • Synergy and network-level response profiling Herbal combinations, fractions, and dose matrices are systematically tested with multi-omic readouts to identify non-additive network responses. Synergy is defined not only by single endpoints such as viability, but by cooperative normalization of lipid, protein, and transcriptional signatures associated with aging and chronic inflammation.
  • Spatially resolved intervention mapping Spatial omics platforms (MALDI, DESI, and related imaging) are used to determine where, within intact tissues and interfaces, herbal interventions restore lipid and protein localization patterns toward a younger or healthier spatial phenotype.
  • Network-guided lead selection and mechanism-of-action Mixture-induced proteomic perturbations, multi-omic signatures, and spatial remodeling are integrated into network pharmacology models that rank natural products and combinations by their ability to rewire disease networks toward homeostatic attractor states.

Together, multi-omics systems biology and herbal systems pharmacology form a closed experimental loop — first defining how lipid–protein networks fail in aging and chronic disease, then evaluating polypharmacological herbal interventions to identify strategies that restore network structure and spatial organization toward healthier physiological states.

Research Interests

My work is grounded in network pharmacology: defining how lipid composition and enzyme networks become dysgregulated in aging and chronic inflammatory disease, and designing multi-target interventions that restore those networks—and their spatial organization—toward healthier system-level states.

My screening strategy is a network-guided phenotypic approach. I first define how lipid–protein networks fail in aging and chronic inflammatory disease, then screen natural products and herbal combinations for their ability to restore these systems-level phenotypes. Proteome-wide stability and structural profiling platforms (CETSA-MS, LiP-MS, PELSA, and related approaches) are then applied under mixture conditions to map how multi-component formulas collectively perturb protein networks, revealing distributed mechanisms of polypharmacological action and emergent synergy.

1. Multi-Omics Systems Biology

Effector-Layer and Spatial Mapping of Lipid–Protein Networks

I use a multi-omics systems biology framework to characterize how lipid metabolism and signaling networks are reorganized across tissues, cell types, and anatomical niches during normal physiology, aging, and chronic inflammatory disease. This framework is anchored in mass spectrometry–based lipidomics and proteomics, with RNA sequencing, cell-type–resolved profiling, and spatial omics providing the regulatory, cellular, and anatomical context needed to interpret these changes at the network level.

  • Lipidomics (LC–MS/MS and imaging): High-resolution LC–MS/MS lipidomics to quantify complex lipid species, combined with class- and species-level ratio analysis to infer in vivo changes in enzymatic activity and pathway flux. These measurements define how lipid composition and bioactive lipid mediators shift across aging and inflammatory states.
  • Proteomics (DIA-MS, protein abundance, PTM, and phosphoproteomics): Quantitative data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry to measure proteome-wide protein abundance with high reproducibility across tissues, cell types, and conditions. This is coupled with targeted and global post-translational modification profiling—including phosphoproteomics—to define kinase activity, inflammatory pathway engagement, protein turnover, and post-translational regulation of lipid metabolic enzymes. Together, these measurements resolve both the quantitative architecture and signaling state of protein networks that govern lipid remodeling in aging and chronic inflammatory disease.
  • Cell-type–resolved profiling (flow cytometry and FACS): Flow cytometric phenotyping and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to isolate defined cell populations prior to lipidomic, proteomic, phosphoproteomic, and transcriptomic analysis. This makes it possible to distinguish compositional shifts in tissue architecture from intrinsic lipid–protein network rewiring within specific immune, stromal, epithelial, or progenitor populations.
  • RNA sequencing: Bulk and sorted-population RNA-seq to capture transcriptional programs that regulate lipid metabolism, stress responses, and inflammatory tone. RNA-seq provides the regulatory scaffold that explains why particular enzymes, transporters, and signaling modules are up- or down-regulated, but is always interpreted in the context of the lipid and protein effectors that directly implement cell state.
  • Spatial omics (MALDI / DESI and related imaging): Mass spectrometry imaging and related spatial omics platforms to map the tissue localization of lipids and proteins within intact organs. These approaches allow direct comparison of molecular localization and remodeling under normal physiology, aging, and pathology, and then assessment of how candidate interventions shift these spatial patterns back toward a younger or healthier phenotype.

I prioritize these layers—lipidomics, proteomics (including protein abundance, PTM, and phosphoproteomics), RNA-seq, cell-type–resolved profiling, and spatial omics—because together they capture the effector layer (lipids and proteins), its regulatory control (transcripts), and its anatomical deployment (spatial localization). Other omics, such as epigenomics or metabolomics, are incorporated as targeted follow-ups when specific mechanistic questions demand them, but the core of my work focuses on functionally and spatially grounded readouts that directly encode network behavior.

Bulk tissue profiling defines organ-level network remodeling; FACS-resolved multi-omics quantifies cell-intrinsic rewiring; and spatial omics preserves architecture, revealing interface- and niche-specific changes that cannot be seen in homogenates. Integrating these data streams enables network models that explain how lipid remodeling, protein signaling state, and spatial organization jointly determine vulnerability or resilience in aging and chronic inflammatory disease, and highlights actionable network nodes and motifs that can be targeted to restore homeostasis.

2. Herbal Systems Pharmacology

Network Pharmacology, Polypharmacology, and Synergy in Herbal Medicine

The second arm of my research applies herbal systems pharmacology to these network maps. Instead of optimizing single-target inhibition, I study how multi-target natural products and rational herbal combinations reshape lipid–protein interaction networks at scale. This adopts an explicit network pharmacology perspective: each compound or formula is modeled as a distributed input into a complex biological graph, influencing multiple nodes and edges simultaneously.

  • Proteome-wide stability and structural profiling (CETSA-MS, LiP-MS, PELSA): Rather than resolving single compound–target pairs, proteome-wide stability and structural profiling platforms (CETSA-MS, LiP-MS, PELSA, and related approaches) are applied under mixture conditions to define the distributed proteomic perturbation landscape induced by multi-component herbal formulas. Ligand-dependent shifts in thermal stability, conformational accessibility, and protein interaction states are quantified across the proteome, capturing both direct binding events and indirect network propagation effects. Instead of reducing complex formulas to isolated targets, these data are integrated to reconstruct polypharmacological network rewiring—identifying cooperatively engaged protein modules and signaling circuits that underlie emergent synergy and systems-level phenotype restoration.
  • Integration with lipidomics and signaling networks: Target engagement and proteome-wide perturbation data are integrated with lipidomic, proteomic, and phosphoproteomic signatures to determine how specific protein modules and interaction states propagate through lipid metabolic and inflammatory circuits. This connects mixture-induced protein perturbations to emergent changes in lipid remodeling, signaling cascades, and effector programs at the network level.
  • Synergy and network-level response profiling: Systematic testing of herbal combinations, fractions, and dose matrices with multi-omic readouts to identify non-additive, network-level responses. Synergy is defined not only by single endpoints such as viability, but by cooperative normalization of multi-node lipid and protein signatures associated with aging and chronic inflammation.
  • Spatially resolved intervention mapping: Leveraging spatial omics (MALDI, DESI, and related imaging) to determine where, within intact tissues and interfaces, herbal interventions restore lipid and protein localization patterns toward a younger or healthier spatial phenotype. This links molecular mechanisms to specific anatomical niches—such as barrier regions, vascular interfaces, and stem/progenitor compartments—that are critical for long-term tissue function.
  • Network-guided lead selection and mechanism-of-action: Integrating mixture-induced proteomic perturbations, multi-omic signatures, and spatial remodeling into network pharmacology models that rank natural products and combinations by their ability to rewire disease networks back toward homeostatic attractor states. This provides a mechanistically grounded path from complex herbal mixtures to prioritized leads and experimentally testable mechanisms of action.

Together, the multi-omics systems biology and herbal systems pharmacology programs form a closed loop: I first use lipidomics, proteomics (including protein abundance, PTM, and phosphoproteomics), RNA-seq, cell-type–resolved profiling, and spatial omics to define how networks fail in aging and chronic inflammatory disease. I then apply polypharmacological and synergistic herbal interventions—quantified by MS-based lipid and protein profiling, CETSA-MS, LiP-MS, PELSA, and spatial readouts—to identify strategies that robustly restore network structure and spatial organization toward healthier phenotypes.

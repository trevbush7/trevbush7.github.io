Mentors

My training is grounded in complementary expertise spanning neural stem cell lipid biology, traditional Chinese medicine and drug development, quantitative proteomics and spatial omics, and systems-level tissue circuitry.

Xiaoai Zhao

Assistant Professor

Departments of Neuroscience and Comparative Medicine, Yale School of Medicine

Research Focus

Dr. Zhao studies how complex lipids regulate neural stem cell function and decline during aging. Her lab dissects how phospholipid and sphingolipid acyl-chain composition shapes organelle biology and cellular stress responses in the aging brain, using mouse neural stem cells as a regenerative model.

Her group maps organelle-specific lipidomic changes across subcellular compartments and investigates how compartmentalized lipid species and lipid-derived metabolites drive functional deterioration in aging and age-related disease. They also probe how complex lipids participate in systems metabolism and tissue–tissue communication, supported by ongoing development of advanced mass spectrometry technologies for tracing tissue-specific lipid metabolites in vivo.

Role in My Training

Dr. Zhao is my primary mentor and the intellectual anchor for my lipidomics work. She provides expertise in experimental design for organelle-resolved and neural-stem-cell–based lipidomics, guidance on linking acyl-chain remodeling to cellular phenotypes, and mentorship in mass spectrometry method development. Her perspective on complex lipids as regulators of systems physiology directly shapes how I frame my research on aging, chronic disease, and neural regeneration.

Yung-Chi Cheng

Henry Bronson Professor of Pharmacology

Department of Pharmacology, Yale School of Medicine

Research Focus

Dr. Cheng is a pioneer in antiviral and anticancer drug development and a global leader in traditional Chinese medicine research. His laboratory developed several clinically important nucleoside analogs and established principles for mechanism-based drug design and toxicity evaluation.

Over the past two decades, his group has focused on traditional Chinese medicine formulas such as PHY906/YIV-906, applying modern pharmacology, mechanistic quality control, and immuno-oncology frameworks to understand how multi-component herbal medicines modulate tumor microenvironments, enhance chemotherapy and checkpoint blockade, and reduce treatment toxicity.

Role in My Training

Dr. Cheng anchors the herbal medicine and translational pharmacology dimensions of my work. From him I gain expertise in traditional Chinese medicine theory, multi-herb formulation design, and mechanism-based quality control, as well as practical insight into clinical translation, regulatory strategy, and interdisciplinary collaboration. His perspective shapes how I build polypharmacological, herb-focused interventions on top of the lipid and proteomic circuits defined in my research.

Yansheng Liu

Associate Professor

Department of Pharmacology & Yale Cancer Center

Research Focus

Dr. Liu’s lab defines quantitative principles of proteome dynamics and proteostasis. Using innovations such as DeltaSILAC, his group maps how post-translational modifications reshape protein turnover, signaling networks, and phenotypes across development, aging, and disease, with particular relevance to cancer and neurodegeneration.

His team also advances DIA-MS workflows, MALDI mass spectrometry imaging, and computational pipelines for PTM and turnover analysis, enabling spatially resolved proteomics, lipidomics, and metabolomics in tissues and clinical samples. Their work seeks universal rules linking genotype, proteotype, and phenotypic variability.

Role in My Training

Dr. Liu provides core expertise in quantitative proteomics, DIA method development, and spatial omics. He advises my integration of lipidomics with proteomic turnover and PTM measurements, and guides the use of MALDI imaging to map lipid and protein circuits in tissues. His training helps me translate lipid-centric findings into quantitative proteostasis models and spatially resolved network maps across aging and chronic disease.

Hattie Chung

Assistant Professor

Department of Pathology & Center for Systems Biology, Harvard Medical School

Research Focus

Dr. Chung’s lab develops predictive frameworks for tissue biology, viewing tissues as dynamic circuits of interacting cells rather than static collections of cell types. Using spatial genomics, multi-omics, and machine-learning–driven models, her group dissects the cell–cell circuit motifs that maintain homeostasis or drive failure across cardiovascular and ovarian systems.

By studying how hormonal cycles, inflammation, and stress are encoded in spatially organized cellular programs, her work seeks general principles of tissue resilience, remodeling, and aging that can be leveraged for new therapeutic strategies.

Role in My Training

Dr. Chung contributes systems biology, spatial modeling, and circuit-level thinking to my dissertation. She helps me frame complex lipid and proteomic data as components of tissue circuits, design experiments that capture cell–cell interactions and spatial context, and apply computational models to distinguish adaptive remodeling from pathological failure. Her mentorship is key for translating my multi-omics data into predictive models of tissue resilience in aging and chronic disease.
